Most organizations do not know how ready they are for AI.

They think they do. They have a strategy document. They have attended a conference. They have run a pilot. But when you sit down with the people doing the actual work, a different picture emerges. Tools purchased and unused. Processes that were supposed to change and did not. Staff who were trained once and never again. A leadership team that believes implementation is further along than it is.

The gap between what leaders believe and what is actually true on the ground is where most implementations fail.

This is the problem I built AI Readiness Levels to address.

## Why existing frameworks fall short

There are plenty of frameworks for AI strategy. Most of them are designed to sell consulting engagements. They are abstract enough to apply to any organization and specific enough to sound useful in a slide deck.

What they do not do is tell you, concretely, where you are right now, what the next step looks like, and what a realistic timeline for getting there is.

The Technology Readiness Level model from NASA does something different. It gives a numbered scale. Each level has specific criteria. You cannot claim to be at level five unless you have demonstrated the things that level five requires. This kind of rigor is uncomfortable for organizations that want to believe they are further along than they are. It is also extremely useful.

AI Readiness Levels applies that logic to organizations.

## What ARL is built on

I developed ARL on top of the PIVOT model, which provides a framework for understanding how organizations transform. PIVOT gives you a way to map where an organization's capacity for change actually sits, separate from where leadership believes it sits. That foundation matters because AI adoption is not a technology problem. It is an organizational change problem that happens to involve technology.

ARL layers a maturity scale on top of that organizational understanding. The levels are defined by concrete criteria, not by self-assessment. You look at what is actually happening: what tools are in use, by whom, how often, with what outcomes. You look at whether accountability structures exist for AI-driven decisions. You look at whether training is ongoing or was a one-time event. You look at whether there is a person in the organization whose job it is to know what is working and what is not.

## What this looks like in practice

When I work with an organization through Arcite-AI, the first thing we do is an ARL assessment. It takes time. We talk to people at every level. We do not rely on what leadership tells us.

What we find, consistently, is that organizations overestimate their readiness by one to two levels. This is not because people are dishonest. It is because the signals of progress are visible and the signals of failure are not. The tool got purchased. That is visible. The fact that sixty percent of the team does not use it is not something that shows up in a leadership report.

The assessment gives us a baseline. It also gives us something more valuable: a shared understanding of what progress actually looks like. Once an organization knows its current level and understands the specific criteria for the next level, the path forward becomes concrete rather than aspirational.

## Why maturity models matter for AI specifically

AI is unusual among organizational technologies because the gap between early adoption and actual integration is long and the failure modes are not obvious.

When a software implementation fails, you know relatively quickly. The system crashes, or the team cannot figure out how to use it, or the data does not flow correctly. The feedback is fast and clear.

When AI adoption fails, it often fails slowly and quietly. The tool works. People use it sometimes. It does not change the underlying process, so the underlying process does not improve. A year later, the organization concludes that AI did not deliver value, and the actual cause, which is that the adoption never got past the surface level, goes unexamined.

ARL gives organizations a way to see this coming before it happens. If you know that your organization is at level two, you know that expecting level five outcomes is unrealistic. You know what needs to happen before those outcomes are accessible. You can plan for it, rather than discovering the gap after you have already spent the money.

## What I am still working on

The framework is in active use at Arcite-AI and through the research I am doing at the Edwards School of Business.

The part I find most interesting right now is validation. It is one thing to define a maturity scale. It is another to demonstrate that organizations assessed at a given level actually show the predicted outcomes. I am working on collecting enough longitudinal data to test whether the framework predicts performance, not just describes it.

That work is ongoing. If you are leading an organization that is trying to figure out where you actually stand with AI, I would be interested in the conversation.
