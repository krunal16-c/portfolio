In 2024, I became president of the University of Saskatchewan Students' Union — a $29M organization representing 19,000 students.

I went in expecting the interesting problems to be policy, advocacy, strategy. They were. But the thing that surprised me most was how many of our hardest decisions were fundamentally questions about trust, accountability, and who gets to make a call when things go wrong.

That framing changed how I think about AI.

## The accountability problem

When something goes wrong in an institution, someone is accountable. That's not just a governance formality — it's load-bearing infrastructure for how organizations maintain legitimacy with the people they serve.

Most AI deployment conversations I see treat accountability as an afterthought. The system fails, someone shrugs, the technical team patches the model. But in an institution that serves 19,000 students, you can't shrug. You have to explain. You have to stand in front of people, take responsibility, and commit to doing better.

AI systems that can't be explained can't be accountable. Systems that can't be accountable shouldn't be making decisions that affect people's lives.

This seems obvious when stated plainly. It's routinely ignored in practice.

## What "adoption" actually requires

The AI industry tends to frame adoption as a technical challenge: get the model right, clean the data, fine-tune the prompts. These matter. But the harder problems are human.

**Trust is earned incrementally.** You don't deploy an AI system for student appeals and ask people to trust it on day one. You build trust by being transparent about what the system does, where it fails, and how the institution responds to those failures. This takes time. It takes iteration. It's not a deployment event.

**Different people need different things.** A communications officer and a finance analyst have different risk tolerances, different workflows, different consequences for errors. Treating "AI training" as a uniform rollout is how you get a compliance checkbox instead of actual capability.

**The people who know most about the problem are usually not the people making the decision.** The staff members doing front-line member services knew more about operational pain points than the board ever would. AI deployment that doesn't center those people will optimize for the wrong things.

## The through-line to Arcite-AI

When I co-founded Arcite-AI, these weren't abstract principles. They were things I'd watched play out concretely.

The reason we built a recommendation system before we built anything custom is exactly this: most organizations don't have clear AI problems — they have unclear organizational problems they're hoping AI will solve. Before you can deploy anything, you have to understand the context. Who's accountable when it fails? Which people will actually use it? What do they actually need?

The Forward Deployed Engineering model exists for the same reason. Sitting in a client's office, watching how they actually work, changes what you build. You stop solving the problem as stated and start solving the problem as lived.

That's not an AI insight. That's an institutional one.

## What I still don't know

The intersection of AI and institutional decision-making is genuinely hard, and the field is moving faster than institutions can adapt.

But I think the researchers and builders who matter most in this space will be the ones who take the human side as seriously as the technical side. That means understanding governance, trust, and accountability — not as constraints on what you can build, but as requirements for building things worth building.
